# Programming methodology

Ranges, not dogma. Where the evidence is genuinely uncertain, this file says so
— pass that uncertainty on rather than inventing precision.

## Evidence posture

Lean on guideline bodies and the weight of the literature:

- **WHO physical activity guidelines** — 150–300 min/week moderate *or*
  75–150 min vigorous aerobic activity, **plus** muscle-strengthening on ≥2
  days/week. This is the floor for general health, not a training program.
- **ACSM** — resistance training 2–3 days/week per muscle group for novices;
  moderate loads (~8–12 reps) build strength and size efficiently in
  untrained people; progressive overload is the governing principle.
- **NSCA** — heavier loads and longer rest for maximal strength; periodized
  programming outperforms non-periodized over long horizons.

**Never cite a specific study you cannot name correctly.** Say "the general
finding across the literature is…" and give the range. A fabricated citation
destroys trust and is worse than no citation at all.

## Choosing the split

Driven by available days and recovery, not by popularity.

| Days/week | Default | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | Full body | Each muscle 2×/week — the minimum effective frequency |
| 3 | Full body | Best frequency-to-volume ratio; ideal for beginners |
| 4 | Upper / Lower ×2 | Each muscle 2×/week with room for more volume per session |
| 5 | Upper/Lower + full body, or PPL+UL | Needs decent recovery capacity |
| 6 | Push/Pull/Legs ×2 | Advanced or well-recovered only; high life cost |

Modifiers:

- **Short sessions (<45 min)** → fewer exercises, more supersets, full-body or
  upper/lower. Never cram 7 exercises into 30 minutes.
- **Strength-primary** → lower reps, longer rest, fewer exercises, more days if
  available. Rest is not optional for strength.
- **Fat loss** → keep resistance training largely intact to preserve muscle;
  create the deficit mostly with nutrition and added activity, not by turning
  lifting into cardio.
- **Sport** → train the quality the sport needs, in-season volume comes *down*,
  and never leave an athlete sore for competition.
- **Body-part splits (1 muscle/week)** are rarely optimal for non-advanced
  lifters — frequency suffers. Use only on 5–6 days with a specific reason.

## Volume

Per muscle group, per week, counting hard sets near failure:

| | Sets/week |
| --- | --- |
| Minimum effective (maintenance) | ~4–6 |
| Beginner | ~8–12 |
| Intermediate hypertrophy | ~12–20 |
| Advanced / specialization | 20+, with care |

More volume generally produces more hypertrophy up to a point, but returns
diminish and fatigue costs rise. **Start at the low end and add** — a program
someone can recover from and repeat beats a maximal one they abandon in three
weeks. Count only hard sets; three easy sets of lateral raises are not three
sets.

Direct and indirect volume both count partially. Rows train biceps; pressing
trains triceps. Don't program 20 direct biceps sets on top of a pulling day.

## Intensity and reps

| Goal | Reps | Load (%1RM) | RIR | Rest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Max strength | 1–5 | 85–95% | 1–3 | 3–5 min |
| Strength–hypertrophy | 5–8 | 75–85% | 1–3 | 2–3 min |
| Hypertrophy | 6–15 | 65–80% | 0–3 | 1.5–3 min |
| Metabolite / isolation | 12–25 | 40–65% | 0–2 | 1–2 min |
| Power | 1–5 | 30–70%, max velocity | — | 2–5 min |
| Muscular endurance | 15–30+ | <60% | 1–3 | 30–90 s |

Two things people get wrong:

1. **Hypertrophy works across a wide load range** when sets are taken close to
   failure — roughly 30–85% of 1RM. Heavy is not required for growth; it *is*
   required for maximal strength.
2. **Rest periods are not a nuisance.** Short rest on compounds reduces the
   load and reps you can perform, cutting the stimulus. 2–3 min on compounds is
   normal, not lazy.

**RIR** = reps in reserve ("RIR 2" = could have done 2 more). Explain it the
first time. It generalizes better than %1RM for people who don't test maxes,
and self-reported RIR tends to be optimistic in beginners — expect their "RIR 2"
to be nearer RIR 4 for the first few months, and say so.

Training to failure is not required for most sets and raises fatigue
disproportionately. Reserve it for isolation work and the last set of an
exercise, if at all.

## Frequency

Hitting a muscle **2×/week** is generally at least as good as 1× when weekly
volume is equal, and makes high volumes easier to tolerate per session. Beyond
2–3×/week the added benefit is unclear at equal volume. Frequency is mainly a
tool for *distributing* volume, not an independent magic variable.

## Progression

**Double progression** is the default. It's simple and it works:

> Prescribe a rep range. Add reps within the range at a fixed load. When you
> hit the top of the range on all sets, add load and drop back to the bottom.

Example: 3×8–12 at 60 kg → 12/12/12 achieved → 65 kg, back to ~8 reps.

Load increments: upper-body isolation 1–2.5 kg · upper compound 2.5 kg ·
lower compound 5 kg. Smaller is better than stalling.

By training age:

- **Beginner** — add load nearly every session while technique holds.
- **Intermediate** — progress weekly; rotate which lifts progress.
- **Advanced** — progress in blocks over months; expect plateaus as normal.

### Stall decision rules

Use the tracker's actual data, never a guess.

| Observed | Do |
| --- | --- |
| Hit top of rep range, all sets, clean technique | Add load |
| Hit top on some sets only | Hold load, accumulate reps another week |
| Same performance 2 weeks running | Hold; check sleep, food, stress first |
| Performance down 2 weeks running | Deload — do not add load |
| Reps down + poor sleep/high stress reported | Recovery problem, not a program problem. Fix inputs before changing the program. |
| Technique degrading as load climbs | Hold load. Form is the cap. |
| Pain (not soreness) | Stop that exercise → `safety.md` |

**Never recommend adding weight purely because a date has passed.** The data
decides.

## Periodization and deloads

Beginners don't need formal periodization — linear progression *is* their
periodization. Introduce structure when progress stops being weekly.

Common models: linear (intensity up, volume down over a block), undulating
(varies session to session), block (accumulation → intensification → peak).
Choose one and keep it legible; a program nobody can follow has no adherence.

**Deload** — roughly every 4–8 weeks, or triggered by: two weeks of declining
performance, persistent joint ache, sleep disruption, or motivation collapse.
Reduce volume to ~half, or keep sets and cut intensity ~10–20%. Keep moving —
a deload is not a week off. Beginners rarely need scheduled deloads; program
them reactively.

## Warm-up

- 5–10 min general: bike, row, brisk walk — raise temperature, not fatigue.
- Dynamic movement for the joints being loaded. Static stretching before
  heavy lifting is unnecessary and may slightly blunt force output; leave it
  for after.
- Ramp-up sets on the first compound: empty bar → ~40% → ~60% → ~80% → working.
  Isolation work doesn't need a ramp.

## Cardio and concurrent training

- WHO floor: 150–300 min/week moderate, or 75–150 vigorous.
- Interference with strength is real but modest and manageable: separate
  hard cardio from lower-body lifting by several hours where possible, prefer
  low-impact modalities, and avoid long hard running the day before heavy legs.
- For fat loss, step count is more sustainable than grinding cardio sessions.
- For general health, cardio is not optional — resistance training alone does
  not cover cardiorespiratory fitness.

## Benchmarks

Give something testable every 4–6 weeks, chosen to match the goal:

- Strength — a rep-max on main lifts (prefer 3–5RM over a true 1RM for
  non-competitors; safer and nearly as informative)
- Hypertrophy — load × reps at fixed RIR, plus tape measurements; scale weight
  is noisy
- Endurance — time, distance, or pace at fixed effort
- General health — resting HR, step count, consistency percentage

Consistency (sessions completed ÷ planned) predicts outcomes better than any
single programming variable. Put it on the dashboard.
